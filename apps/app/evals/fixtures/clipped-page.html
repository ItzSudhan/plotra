How canal cities hold back the tide

Cities built below the high-water line survive on three things: a barrier, a pump and a habit. The barrier is the part visitors photograph. The habit is the part that fails first.

The oldest surviving tide gates are timber mitre gates, hung in pairs so that the weight of the water presses them shut. They are opened at slack water, when the levels on both sides agree, and closed again before the flood. A gate opened ten minutes late can cost a street.

Pumping stations

Where the ground sinks faster than the sea rises, gates are not enough. Pumping stations lift water out of the low canals into a ring canal that drains at ebb. Before steam, the lifting was done by wind and by treadwheel; a city of forty thousand needed about sixty mills.

Subsidence

Most canal cities are sinking, by a few millimetres a year, because the peat and clay under them is drying out. Measured against the walls, a sinking city and a rising sea look exactly the same. Engineers tell them apart with benchmarks cut into bedrock outside the city.

What fails

Records from six cities show the same pattern. The barrier is rarely overtopped. What fails is the watch: a keeper asleep, a signal not passed on, a table of tides copied wrong. The remedy in every case was to make the routine public, so that many people would notice when it slipped.
