A beta reader gives you the one thing you can no longer get from your own draft: a first read. You know what every scene is supposed to do, so you cannot see what it actually does. Here is how to work with beta readers so that their first read becomes a revision plan.

## What a beta reader is for

A beta reader reports a reaction. They tell you where they were hooked, where they were lost, and where they put the book down to make tea and did not hurry back.

A beta reader is not an editor.

| | Beta reader | Editor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gives you | How the book felt to read | Why it reads that way, and what to change |
| Works on | The whole story, at reading speed | The text, slowly, line by line |
| Good answer | "I skimmed the Friar's speech in the tomb" | "The speech retells four scenes the reader has just watched" |

When a beta reader says a scene is slow, believe them. Readers are reliable about the symptom and unreliable about the cure.

## When a draft is ready, and who to ask

Send the draft when you have run out of problems you can see yourself. If you already know the middle sags, a reader telling you so teaches you nothing. [Find plot holes and continuity errors](/blog/how-to-avoid-plot-holes) before a reader does, or every note you get back will be about them.

Do not polish the prose first. Beta feedback moves and cuts scenes, and a cut scene does not need perfect commas.

Ask three to six people. With fewer than three you cannot tell one person's taste from a pattern. With many more, you sort notes that repeat each other. Choose for range:

- **At least two people who read your genre for pleasure.** They know what the book is promising.
- **One person who reads widely but not in your genre.** They catch what genre readers forgive.
- **Nobody who has heard you talk through the plot.** They cannot have a first read. They already know Juliet is not dead.

Keep one or two readers in reserve for the next draft. A reader can only meet the book once.

## What to tell readers before they start

Write one short message and send the same one to everyone:

1. **What it is.** Genre, length, and that it is a draft that has not been copy edited.
2. **What you want.** Their reaction as a reader. Say plainly that they do not need to fix typos or suggest solutions.
3. **How to give it.** Mark the passage and write a few words while reading, not a report afterwards.
4. **The date.** A real one, three or four weeks out for a novel.
5. **Permission to stop.** If they give up, you need to know the page. That is the most useful note they can send.

Do not explain the book or warn them about the part you are worried about.

## Ask for reactions at the passage level

A summary written a week after finishing is a memory of the book, tidied up. A mark on the exact paragraph is evidence. Compare two kinds of note on a draft of Romeo and Juliet:

| Summary note | Passage note |
| --- | --- |
| "The ending felt rushed." | On Friar John's quarantine: "Wait, so the letter just never arrived? I had to reread this." |
| "Some parts were slow." | On the Friar's speech in the tomb: "Skimmed. I watched all this happen." |
| "I liked Mercutio." | On Mercutio's death: "Did not see this coming. Read the next three scenes without stopping." |

The second column tells you which paragraph to open. Ask readers for three marks above all: where they were **bored**, where they were **confused**, and where they **could not stop**. The third tells you what not to touch.

## A beta reader questionnaire you can copy

Passage marks show you where. A short questionnaire at the end shows you the whole shape. Ask about the reader's experience, never about solutions.

1. Where did you first put the book down, and what made you pick it up again?
2. At what point, if any, did you feel you had to keep reading?
3. Was there a place where you skimmed? Which chapter?
4. Was anything confusing: who someone was, where a scene took place, how much time had passed?
5. Which character would you follow into another book? Which one could you not picture?
6. Was there a moment when a character did something you did not believe they would do?
7. What did you expect to happen that did not?
8. Which scene do you remember best, a week later?
9. Did the ending feel earned? Rate it from 1 to 5.
10. How did the pace feel overall: too slow, about right, or too fast?
11. In one sentence, what is this book about?
12. Who would you give this book to?

Question 11 is the quiet test. If four readers give four different answers, the book has not yet decided what it is.

With the module on, build this once in the **Reviews** view: open the **Questionnaires** tab and press **New questionnaire**. Set **About** to the whole project, or to one chapter or folder. Each question is a **Written answer**, a **Rating** from 1 to 5, or a **Multiple choice**, so questions 9 and 10 become one click each. **Results** shows every answer beside its author, the average for a rating and a count for each choice. See [questionnaires](/docs/modules/editorial#questionnaires).

## How to read conflicting feedback and decide what to change

Readers will disagree. Sort every note into one of four piles before you change a word:

| What you see | What it means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Several readers mark the same passage, same reaction | A real problem | Fix it |
| Several mark the same passage, opposite reactions | The passage is doing something strong | Decide which reader the book is for, then leave it or sharpen it |
| One reader, one mark | Taste, or an early warning | Note it. Change it only if you agree |
| Readers agree on the fix | Still only a symptom report | Find the cause yourself |

Take the second row. One reader marks Romeo and Juliet's first meeting "too fast, I do not believe it". Another marks the same lines "loved it". Do not split the difference and slow the scene halfway. The speed is the point: the whole story takes five days. The useful question is whether the first reader was told early enough that this is a story about haste.

Then write the plan as a short list of causes, not a long list of notes, and work from the largest change to the smallest.

Before the rewrite, save the draft your readers read. In Plotra, open **History** in the inspector, type a name such as "Before the rewrite", and click **Save**. Named versions are kept until someone deletes them. A version is a copy of one note, not of the whole project, so save one for each chapter you are about to rework. See [version history](/docs/collaboration/version-history).

When the story is settled and you bring in an editor, share the project as **Editor** and ask them to turn on suggesting mode. Every insertion and deletion is marked until you **Accept** or **Reject** it. A commenter cannot suggest. See [suggestions](/docs/collaboration/comments-and-suggestions#suggestions). The same setup works if you [co-write a novel](/blog/how-to-co-write-a-novel).

A reader sees the whole project: every note, including your character pages, your outline and anything marked as not yet revealed. There are no permissions on single notes or folders, and a commenter can export what they can open.

## What to do next

Write the message from the third section today, before you choose your readers. If you cannot say in two lines what you want from them, the draft is not ready to send. Then copy the twelve questions, cut the ones that do not fit your book, and pick a date.
